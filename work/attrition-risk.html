Ranking employees by attrition risk finds twice as many leavers as chance

Canterra loses about 600 of its 4,000 employees a year. My model ranks every employee by risk: a list of the riskiest 1,000 would include about 320 of the year's leavers, against 150 for a random list of the same size.

Context
Advanced Machine Learning and AI, Georgetown MSBA, November 2026. Course dataset of 1,470 employees.
Role
Sole author
Methods
Random forest and neural network with a logistic-regression benchmark, cross-validation, permutation importance, partial dependence
Tools
Python (pandas, scikit-learn)

Problem

Canterra loses about 15% of its workforce every year. HR can't meet all 4,000 employees and had no way to tell who was about to leave. The questions were who is most likely to go, why, and what management can do about it.

Approach

The most important decision came before any model. The file listed every employee three times under different IDs, so a random split put copies of the same person in both the training and the test data. With the copies kept, 90% of test rows had a twin in training and the random forest scored an AUC of 0.95. After collapsing the file to 1,470 real people, the honest test AUC was about 0.67, and 0.73 on average across ten different splits.

From there I tuned a random forest and a neural network with five-fold cross-validation, selecting on AUC because accuracy is meaningless when 84% of employees stay, and used the test set once. A plain logistic regression matched both, a sign that the drivers act in a fairly simple, additive way. I recommended the network because it overfit less and varied less across splits.

Result

Scaled to Canterra, the riskiest 1,000 employees would include about 320 of the year's 600 leavers, twice what a random list of the same size reaches. The drivers are mostly things management controls: frequent business travel (25% predicted risk against 13% for employees who don't travel) and low job or environment satisfaction. Pay made no meaningful difference.

I recommended a quarterly risk list for targeted retention conversations, a review of frequent travel, and a random comparison group kept for two quarters so the program's real effect can be measured before it is scaled.

Robustness and limits

A version without age, gender and marital status ranks slightly worse (AUC 0.72 against 0.76 in cross-validation) but never flags anyone for who they are, so it's the one I recommended using. The drivers are associations, not causes, and with 71 leavers in the test set results move between splits. Resignations and dismissals are combined, and the file has no overtime, performance or promotion data.

What I would do differently

Ask for better data before chasing a better model. Gradient boosting, a three-model average and engineered features all landed within noise of the same ceiling, around 0.75. Overtime, promotions, raises and exit reasons would move that ceiling more than any tuning.
